Group research project · TU Delft

LLM annotation panel

Do language models annotate more consistently when they can see how their peers annotated? A panel of LLMs labelling creative thinking in real meeting transcripts.

Overview

Annotating creative thinking in conversations is slow and subjective work for people. This project tests whether a panel of large language models can do it consistently, and whether the models agree more when each one can see how the others annotated before, in the spirit of a panel of experts that calibrates over time.

Set-up

  • Data. 232 meeting transcripts from the QMSum dataset (academic, committee and product meetings). Speech-recognition markup is cleaned and the first 100 speaker turns of each meeting are kept.
  • Codebook. Eight types of creative thinking, each with a definition and an example: divergent thinking, convergent thinking, fluency, flexibility, elaboration, associative leap, contextual sensitivity and problem recognition.
  • Prompt. Each model gets an expert-annotator instruction, the codebook and the transcript, and must return pure JSON: a list of exact transcript passages, each with a creative type.
  • Two conditions. In each iteration a new transcript is drawn and every model annotates it twice. In the baseline condition it works alone. In the feedback condition its prompt also contains the other two models' annotations of the previous transcript, as few-shot examples.
  • Runs. Five independent runs of about forty iterations each.

Measuring agreement

Each annotated passage is located in the transcript by an exact word-sequence match. Two annotations agree when they carry the same label and their word spans overlap. True positives, false positives and false negatives are summed over every pair of models, giving one micro-averaged F1 per iteration and condition. The conditions are compared with a paired t-test after Shapiro-Wilk normality checks, and Kendall's tau tests for a trend over iterations.

Seeing their peers' annotations made the models agree more (paired t-test, t(38) = 4.20, p < 0.001), and the gain held in four of the five runs. Agreement did not trend up or down over the iterations in either condition, so the effect comes from the examples in the prompt rather than from a gradual drift.

Agreement is not accuracy, though: a panel can converge on the same wrong label. An automated annotator like this needs a small human-verified set to calibrate against before it is trusted at scale.

My part

I wrote the experiment loop that runs the panel over the iterations and both conditions and passes the previous annotations around, and most of the prompt definitions. Other team members built the model wrappers, preprocessing, the agreement metric, the plots and the statistical analysis.
