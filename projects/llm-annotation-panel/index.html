Group research project · TU Delft

LLM annotation panel

Do language models annotate more consistently when they can see how their peers annotated? A panel of LLMs labelling creative thinking in real meeting transcripts.

Overview

Annotating creative thinking in conversations is slow and subjective work for people. This project tests whether a panel of large language models can do it consistently, and whether the models agree more when each one can see how the others annotated before, in the spirit of a panel of experts that calibrates over time.

The panel has three members: Llama 3.3 70B (served through Groq), GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, each accessed through its provider's API.

Set-up

  • Data. 232 meeting transcripts from the QMSum dataset (academic, committee and product meetings), cleaned of speech-recognition markup and truncated to the first 100 speaker turns.
  • Codebook. Eight types of creative thinking, each with a definition and an example: divergent thinking, convergent thinking, fluency, flexibility, elaboration, associative leap, contextual sensitivity and problem recognition.
  • Prompting. Each model receives an expert-annotator instruction, the codebook and the transcript, and must return structured JSON: a list of verbatim transcript passages, each labelled with a creative type.
  • Two conditions. In each iteration a new transcript is drawn and every model annotates it twice. In the baseline condition it works alone. In the feedback condition its prompt also contains the other two models' annotations of the previous transcript as few-shot examples, never its own.
  • Runs. Five independent runs of about forty iterations each.

Experiment design

Both conditions annotate the same transcript in every iteration, and only feedback-condition answers are carried forward as peer examples. The two conditions therefore differ only in what is in the prompt, which makes them directly comparable. The first iteration of each run has no previous annotations, so its baseline answers seed the peer examples for the second. Annotations are stored per model, iteration and condition, so the analysis can be rerun without calling the models again.

Measuring agreement

Each annotated passage is located in the transcript by exact word-sequence matching, which turns it into a span of word positions. Two annotations agree when they carry the same label and their spans overlap; each annotation can be matched only once. True positives, false positives and false negatives are summed over every pair of models, giving one micro-averaged pairwise F1 per iteration and condition as the inter-annotator agreement measure.

Scores are averaged per iteration over the five runs. The conditions are compared with a paired t-test after Shapiro-Wilk normality checks, and Kendall's tau tests for a trend over iterations.

Seeing their peers' annotations made the models agree more (paired t-test, t(38) = 4.20, p < 0.001), and the gain held in four of the five runs. Agreement showed no trend over the iterations in either condition, so the effect comes from the examples in the prompt rather than from a gradual drift.

Agreement is not accuracy: a panel can converge on the same wrong label. An automated annotator like this needs a small human-verified set to calibrate against before it is used at scale.

My part

I designed and wrote the experiment loop that runs the panel over the iterations and both conditions and routes the peer annotations between models, including running both conditions on the same transcript in every iteration, and wrote most of the prompts. Other team members built the model interfaces, preprocessing, the agreement metric, the plots and the statistical analysis.
