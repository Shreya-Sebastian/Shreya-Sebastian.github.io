Earthquake visualizer

An interactive web visualisation of significant earthquakes and tsunamis, in which a map, a timeline, a scatterplot and a detail panel filter each other.

Try it live. Open the earthquake visualizer ↗

Overview

The application lets people explore historical records of 6,393 significant earthquakes and 2,512 tsunamis, from ancient records to 2025. It has four linked views, so a selection in any of them filters the others. The sections below explain how the views are connected and then walk through each one.

How the views are linked

Each view has two operations, draw from scratch and update with a new set of earthquakes. A view that handles a selection calls the update of the other three with the selected records, and each receiving view uses them in its own way:

Receiving viewWhat it does with a selection
World mapShows only the selected earthquakes, in both the point and the heatmap layer.
ScatterplotKeeps every earthquake in the background and draws the selected ones on top as larger blue points.
TimelineRedraws the grid from the selected earthquakes, so its year ranges adapt to the span of the selection.
Detail viewShows the full record of the first selected earthquake.

A reset button and a tsunami-only switch above the views act on all four at once.

View 1 · my part

Timeline

The records span several thousand years and are very unevenly spread, so a simple histogram over time does not work. I built the timeline as a grid: rows are ranges of years, columns are months, and each cell is coloured by the number of earthquakes.

The range width follows the span of the data shown: 500 years when it covers more than 500 years, then centuries, decades and finally single years once the span is ten years or less.

  • Dragging a box over cells selects a timeframe and filters the map, scatterplot and detail view to those earthquakes. Selected cells turn orange with an outline, and a line under the grid says how many earthquakes are selected, or that the selection is empty. A code comment notes that the outline keeps a selection visible even where the cells are empty, and an empty selection also resets the other views.
  • Clicking a year range zooms into it with finer ranges, and a back button returns to the previous level. The timeline keeps a history of the data it has shown, and the back button is disabled at the top level.
  • 402 records, mostly historical, have no month. They get their own Unknown column, set slightly apart, which can be selected like any month.
  • Colours follow a log scale, so year ranges with only a few records stay visible next to the busiest ones. The palette runs from yellow through green to blue, empty cells stay neutral grey, and selections switch to a contrasting orange and red palette.

The grid, zoom, back navigation and brush filtering date from the course. The log-scaled colours, the outline and status line, the reset on an empty selection and the selectable Unknown column were added in my fork afterwards, as the repository's README lists; before that, earthquakes without a recorded month were left out of every timeline selection.

View 2

World map

Earthquakes are drawn as points over tectonic plate boundaries. Dot size and colour can be mapped to magnitude, focal depth, intensity, deaths, injuries, damage or houses destroyed, and colour can also show the country or whether the earthquake caused a tsunami.

  • The OpenLayers map stacks four layers: an OpenStreetMap base map, the plate boundaries as red lines, a heatmap that is hidden by default, and the earthquake points. Dot size grows linearly with magnitude and intensity and logarithmically with depth and the impact measures.
  • A switch turns the points into a heatmap, and another shows only earthquakes that caused a tsunami. The heatmap weights each earthquake by its magnitude, scaled down to avoid oversaturation according to the code comment.
  • Clicking or Ctrl-dragging a box on the map selects earthquakes there. The box selection follows the OpenLayers box-selection example, including boxes that cross the 180th meridian.
  • Here the map shows only the earthquakes from the timeline selection above.

View 3

Scatterplot

Any two attributes can be plotted against each other; by default it shows magnitude against focal depth, where most earthquakes cluster at shallow depths and a separate group of deep-focus earthquakes stands out.

  • Both axes can be switched to impact measures such as deaths or damage, on a log scale where needed. In D3 terms, an axis becomes a symmetric log scale with ticks at powers of ten once its largest value exceeds 1,000. Earthquakes missing a value for one of the two attributes are left out.
  • Clicking or Ctrl-dragging selects earthquakes, which are then highlighted on the map and timeline. Changing an axis keeps the selection highlighted, and a message appears if the selected earthquakes have no value for the new axes.

Points of interest and details

Three preset points of interest lead into notable patterns: earthquakes with many people missing, earthquakes with many houses destroyed, and low-magnitude earthquakes that caused a lot of damage. Each preset comes with a short explanation and a link to official information about the event. Selecting a single earthquake shows its full record in the detail panel, including its location, magnitude, depth, impact figures and any tsunamis it caused.

Each preset selects one event in all four views and sets the scatterplot axes to match the pattern, for example magnitude against damage for the low-magnitude case. In the detail view, date and time fields are combined into one timestamp, an impact figure that is missing is replaced by its recorded category where there is one, and an earthquake's tsunami identifier is used to look up the related tsunami record and show it in a second section.

Data

The earthquake and tsunami records were exported as tab-separated files and converted to GeoJSON with a small Python script that keeps every row with valid coordinates as a point feature. The application is built with D3 for the timeline, scatterplot and detail view, OpenLayers for the map and heatmap, and Vite for builds. After the course I added a GitHub Pages deployment to my fork, so every push to the main branch builds and publishes the site.
