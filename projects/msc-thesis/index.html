Social inclusion from sensor data

Automated discovery of latent activity contexts from multimodal sensing, to analyse peer interaction of preschoolers with hearing loss in an inclusive classroom.

The problem

In inclusive classrooms, the physical presence of children with hearing loss (HL) does not guarantee social inclusion with their typically hearing (TH) peers. Self-report is biased, and manual observation is too labour-intensive to cover a full school day at fine temporal resolution.

Inclusion is also not a stable trait of a child: it varies with what the class is doing. A daily average over context-specific effects of opposite sign can hide both. The thesis therefore labels every child-minute with an activity context discovered from the sensors, and compares HL and TH children within each context.

Research questions

RQ1. Can interpretable latent activity contexts be recovered from fused ultra-wideband (UWB) spatial data and LENA acoustic data using unsupervised clustering?

RQ2. Within each context, do HL and TH children differ on three sensor-derivable markers of inclusion: peer co-presence, vocal participation rate and peer affiliation patterns?

Data

One inclusive preschool classroom with 13 children (6 HL, 7 TH), recorded over 13 days for about 164 minutes per child per day. Each child wears a vest with two UWB tags, giving position and shoulder orientation at 10 Hz, and a LENA recorder whose audio is classified into speaker-type segments. Orientation comes from the line joining the two tags, so it describes the body, not the head or gaze. After fusing the two streams, 21,847 attendance-confirmed child-minutes are used for the statistics. A manual coding of the daily routine into eight activity types is used only as a loose reference when interpreting the contexts, never to fit the clustering.

Pipeline

The two streams are fused because neither is sufficient alone: position is needed to see co-presence and grouping, and audio to measure vocal participation. The output is one row per (child, minute), labelled with an activity context.

UWB (10 Hz) ─► displacement, teacher distance, spatial groups (Dominant Sets) ─┐
                                                                             ├─► join per (child, minute)
LENA audio  ─► adult words, overlap, utterances per minute ──────────────────┘
   ─► room-level mean (≥ 3 children) ─► Yeo-Johnson ─► GMM, stability-aware K ─► LMM per context
  • Spatial groups. Children who are simultaneously close and mutually body-oriented are grouped with the Dominant Sets framework, on the product of a proximity term (σ = 0.5 m) and a mutual-orientation term.
  • Room-level features. Four features shared by everyone in the room are averaged over the children present each minute: adult word count, auditory overlap, cumulative displacement and teacher distance. The behavioural outcomes are kept out of the clustering to avoid circularity.
  • Context discovery. A full-covariance Gaussian Mixture Model on Yeo-Johnson transformed features, with the number of contexts chosen by a stability-aware BIC rule.
  • Inference. A linear mixed model with a random intercept per child, with per-context Wald contrasts between HL and TH children, Mann-Whitney U tests on a homophily index, and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction within each marker.

Method and design choices

Spatial features and groups

Per-frame displacement is the distance between consecutive 100 ms samples, summed over the minute. It is deliberately not a velocity, since dividing by the short sample interval would mainly amplify position noise, and displacements across gaps longer than 5 s are zeroed, because such jumps are usually a child leaving and re-entering the tracked area.

Spatial groups follow the procedure and implementation of an earlier pipeline for the same cohort, adopted unchanged. For each frame a pairwise affinity A_ij = exp(−d_ij² / 2σ²) × max(0, u_ij·d_i) × max(0, −u_ij·d_j) multiplies a proximity term by a term that is non-zero only when both children face along the line between them. With σ = 0.5 m the proximity term is about 0.61 at 0.5 m, 0.14 at 1 m and 0.01 at 1.5 m, so it recognises children within personal distance, scaled to preschoolers, and keeps the definition comparable with the earlier study. The product form means that two children who are close but back to back, or facing each other from several metres away, both score zero; an additive form would let either cue alone produce a high affinity. Groups are extracted as dominant sets of this matrix by replicator dynamics, which is preferred to a partitioning method such as k-means because groups vary in size and not every child has to belong to one. Each group is then labelled HL-only, TH-only or mixed.

Acoustic features and fusion

LENA segments are short (median 1.05 s) and often straddle a minute boundary, so each segment's adult words, utterances and overlap are split across minutes in proportion to the time falling in each, which conserves totals. The two tables are joined on (child, minute) as a complete case, so inclusion is never inferred from one modality when the other is missing. The one-minute epoch spans many conversational turns, so acoustic features reflect the activity rather than where turn boundaries fall, while staying short enough not to blur distinct phases of the day.

Room-level features

A child's behaviour is only interpretable relative to the concurrent activity, so each minute is described by the mean of four features over the children present. They cover the four moderators of communicative demand identified in the literature (adult vocal input, acoustic demand, physical activity and adult proximity), they are shared by everyone in the room, and they exclude the outcomes. Time of day was rejected because it would leak the routine's sequence into the cluster definition. Minutes with fewer than three children are excluded: with one or two children the "room" mean is just individual or pair behaviour, and three is the smallest count at which it differs from any single child.

Three of the four features are right-skewed (skewness +1.13, +2.57 and +11.10 for adult words, displacement and teacher distance), so a per-feature Yeo-Johnson transformation is applied before clustering. Box-Cox cannot handle the zeros in displacement and overlap, a fixed log(1+x) would correct features with very different skews unequally, and standardisation alone does not change distribution shape.

Gaussian mixture and the choice of K

A full-covariance GMM is used rather than k-means because it gives posterior memberships, suited to gradual classroom transitions, and models correlated features such as high adult speech with low movement. At K = 6 its mean BIC is 21,203 against 21,792 for diagonal and 22,692 for spherical covariance. Minutes are hard-assigned to their most probable component, which costs little: the median posterior of the assigned component is 0.90 and only 5.9% of minutes fall below 0.5. For each K from 2 to 20, twenty GMMs are fitted with independent seeds and ten restarts each.

BIC alone keeps finding shallow minima that reflect fitting noise. So a value of K is admissible only if its partition reproduces across random initialisations (mean pairwise ARI of at least 0.80), and the admissible K with the lowest mean BIC is chosen. That gives K = 6. K = 7 has a marginally lower BIC but does not reproduce across seeds (ARI 0.69). The choice holds for any threshold between 0.70 and 0.85.

Statistical inference

Each child contributes many correlated minutes, so each outcome is modelled with a linear mixed model: cluster, hearing status and their interaction as fixed effects, and a random intercept per child. Random slopes were not estimated, since with 13 children and six clusters they would be under-identified. A Mann-Whitney check first confirms that HL and TH children spend comparable shares of the day in each context. The homophily index is one value per child per context and is tested with Mann-Whitney U, since six against seven children cannot support a normality assumption. Benjamini-Hochberg correction is applied within each marker's six contrasts: the markers are separate constructs, and a family-wise correction such as Bonferroni would cost too much power in an exploratory study of 13 children.

Findings

Peer co-presence

The difference changes sign between contexts. HL children are in groups more than TH children during independent work (+7.8% of the minute, d = +1.59, q < 0.0001), and less during peer-driven activity (−4.3%, q = 0.02) and seated guided work (−5.3%, q = 0.002). Pooled over the day the two groups look the same (38.8% against 39.9%), because these effects cancel out.

Vocal participation

HL children speak less in the contexts where fast peer talk dominates and adult scaffolding is reduced: peer-driven activity (q = 0.034) and adult-scaffolded peer activity (q = 0.048), with dispersed transition in the same direction but marginal. Where adult speech dominates or the room is quiet, the gap is small and not significant.

Peer affiliation

The most consistent asymmetry. TH children's grouped time is concentrated in TH-only groups, while HL children spend more grouped time in mixed groups than TH children in all six contexts, significantly in four. The class composition alone (6 HL, 7 TH) gives TH children a homophily advantage of about 0.08, since a TH child has 6 same-status classmates out of 12 and an HL child 5; in the four significant contexts the observed gap is 0.12 to 0.19. The asymmetry is as large in teacher-organised contexts as in child-chosen ones.

What it shows

Conditioning on context reveals effects that a whole-day comparison averages away. The per-child random intercept matters for the same reason: a simpler ANOVA would report no effect of hearing status on vocal participation, while the mixed model shows that the signal is concentrated in specific contexts. The results were presented to developmental psychologists, who were interested in what the findings mean for classroom practice.

Limitations

  • A single classroom with 13 children; the contexts and effect directions need replication across sites.
  • Orientation comes from the shoulders, not gaze, so group membership carries some measurement error. The orientation term also departs from the published detector for this cohort, which uses proximity alone.
  • Each child contributes about a tenth of the room average that labels a minute, a weak coupling between context discovery and comparison.
  • The markers are proxies: they indicate exclusion risk, not whether a child's bids for interaction are taken up.

Data access

The recordings are child-worn audio and indoor tracking of minors, collected under a research-ethics protocol that does not permit public release. The repository contains the analysis pipeline only.
