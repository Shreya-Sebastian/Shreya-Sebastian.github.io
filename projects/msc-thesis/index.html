MSc thesis · TU Delft

Social inclusion from sensor data

Automated discovery of latent activity contexts from multimodal sensing, to analyse peer interaction of preschoolers with hearing loss in an inclusive classroom.

Summary

  • My MSc thesis measures the social inclusion of preschool children with hearing loss from wearable sensor data, comparing them with typically hearing peers within each classroom activity context rather than over a whole day.
  • A review of the developmental literature on pragmatic language and modality mismatch motivates the context features and three hypotheses stated in advance.
  • Ultra-wideband positions and LENA audio are fused into 21,847 child-minutes, spatial groups are found as dominant sets of an orientation-aware affinity matrix, and six activity contexts are discovered with a Gaussian Mixture Model whose K is chosen by a stability-aware BIC rule.
  • Linear mixed models with per-context contrasts and false discovery rate correction show effects that change sign between contexts and cancel out over the day, such as children with hearing loss spending more time in groups during independent work (d = +1.59) and less during peer-driven activity.
  • The most consistent finding is peer affiliation: children with hearing loss spend more grouped time in mixed groups than their typically hearing peers in all six contexts, significantly in four.

Motivation

In inclusive classrooms, the physical presence of children with hearing loss (HL) does not guarantee their social inclusion with typically hearing (TH) peers. Self-report is subject to bias, and manual observation cannot cover a full school day at fine temporal resolution. Inclusion is also expected to vary with the ongoing classroom activity, so a daily average may conceal context-specific effects of opposite sign. The thesis therefore labels every child-minute with an activity context discovered from wearable sensors, and compares HL and TH children within each context.

RQ1. Can interpretable latent activity contexts be recovered from fused ultra-wideband (UWB) spatial data and LENA acoustic data using unsupervised clustering?

RQ2. Within each context, do HL and TH children differ on three sensor-derivable markers of inclusion: peer co-presence, vocal participation rate and peer affiliation patterns?

Background and related work

The thesis opens with a review of the developmental, audiological and computational literature, which motivates both the choice of context features and the hypotheses tested. Four strands of that review are summarised below.

Peer interaction depends on the voice

Much of the social meaning in children's peer interaction is carried by the voice, not only by words: pitch, intonation, speech rate and loudness signal emotion and intent. These cues are hard to access with hearing loss. Cochlear implant users, for example, recognise vocal emotion above chance but less reliably than TH listeners. Children with HL also often show delays in pragmatic language, the social use of communication such as turn-taking and inferring a speaker's intention, even when other language skills are age-appropriate (Mood et al., 2020). Such delays are linked to higher rates of isolation and peer rejection (Antia et al., 2011; Xie et al., 2014), and Lin et al. (2019) found that pragmatic language, rather than disability status itself, predicts how often children play across groups.

Modality mismatch

A central idea in the review is modality mismatch: the dominant channel of a peer group, usually fast spoken exchange among TH children, is not equally accessible to every child. Observational studies describe HL children moving towards physical play in the noisy playground (Da Silva et al., 2022), and producing far fewer initiated comments than hearing peers while still maintaining turns once an exchange has begun (Bobzien et al., 2013). On this view, an apparent preference for non-verbal play is partly a property of the setting rather than a stable trait of the child.

Communicative demand varies with the activity

Classroom activities differ in how much they demand of a listener. Irvin et al. (2021) found that the rate of vocal interaction changes with the area of the classroom children occupy, and children with HL need considerably better signal-to-noise ratios than their peers to follow speech in reverberant, multi-talker rooms (Iglehart, 2019). The review identifies four recurring axes of this demand: the amount of adult speech, overlapping talk and noise, physical movement, and the teacher's proximity. These became the four features used to discover activity contexts.

Measuring interaction with sensors

Wearable sensing has been used to measure peer interaction at a scale that observation cannot reach. Eichengreen et al. (2024) used proximity sensors to reveal schoolyard participation differences for deaf and hard-of-hearing pupils that peer nominations missed, and LENA has become the standard for child-worn audio. Fasano et al. (2021) and Irvin et al. (2021) combined vocalisation and position data to study inclusive classrooms. Spatial groups are detected with the Dominant Sets framework, building on earlier work by the same research group on homophily in this cohort (Tian et al., 2025). Most of these studies, however, report a single outcome aggregated over the whole observation period, and few model how communicative demand changes during the day. That gap is the starting point of this thesis.

Hypotheses, stated in advance from the literature:

H1. Peer co-presence shows no consistent HL deficit; where differences appear, HL children match or exceed TH children in contexts of low communicative demand.

H2. The HL/TH gap in vocal participation depends on context, and is largest where fast multiparty peer talk dominates with little adult mediation.

H3. TH children affiliate more with same-status peers than HL children do, and this asymmetry is larger in activities where children choose their own groups (McPherson et al., 2001).

Data

One inclusive preschool classroom with 13 children (6 HL, 7 TH), recorded over 13 days for about 164 minutes per child per day. Each child wears two UWB tags, giving position and shoulder orientation at 10 Hz, and a LENA recorder whose audio is classified into speaker-type segments. After multimodal fusion, 21,847 attendance-confirmed child-minutes enter the analysis.

Pipeline

UWB (10 Hz) -> displacement, teacher distance, spatial groups (Dominant Sets) --+
                                                                              +-> join per (child, minute)
LENA audio  -> adult words, overlap, utterances per minute ---------------------+
   -> room-level mean (≥ 3 children) -> Yeo-Johnson -> GMM, stability-aware K -> LMM per context

Position is needed to measure co-presence and grouping, and audio to measure vocal participation, so the two streams are joined on (child, minute) as a complete case. LENA segments that straddle a minute boundary are split proportionally, which conserves totals. Displacement is summed per minute rather than converted to a velocity, which would amplify position noise.

Method

Spatial groups

Following an earlier pipeline for the same cohort, each frame gets a pairwise affinity A_ij = exp(−d_ij² / 2σ²) × max(0, u_ij·d_i) × max(0, −u_ij·d_j), the product of a Gaussian proximity kernel (σ = 0.5 m, personal distance scaled to preschoolers) and a mutual-orientation term. The product form means that children who are close but back to back, or facing each other from several metres away, score zero. Groups are extracted as dominant sets of the affinity matrix by replicator dynamics, preferred over partitioning methods such as k-means because groups vary in size and not every child must belong to one. Each group is labelled HL-only, TH-only or mixed.

Context features

Each minute is described by four room-level features averaged over the children present: adult word count, auditory overlap, cumulative displacement and teacher distance. They cover the moderators of communicative demand identified in the literature, are shared by everyone in the room, and exclude the behavioural outcomes to avoid circularity. Time of day is excluded to avoid leaking the routine into the clusters, and minutes with fewer than three children are dropped. Because the features are strongly right-skewed and contain zeros, a per-feature Yeo-Johnson transformation is applied rather than Box-Cox or a fixed log transform.

Context discovery

A full-covariance Gaussian Mixture Model is used rather than k-means because it gives posterior memberships, suited to gradual classroom transitions, and models correlated features. It also has a lower BIC than diagonal and spherical covariance. Hard assignment costs little: the median posterior of the assigned component is 0.90.

Since BIC alone tends to settle on shallow minima that reflect fitting noise, K is selected with a stability-aware rule. For each K from 2 to 20, twenty GMMs are fitted with independent seeds. A K is admissible only if its partitions agree across seeds (mean pairwise adjusted Rand index of at least 0.80), and the admissible K with the lowest mean BIC is chosen. This gives K = 6, and the choice holds for any threshold between 0.70 and 0.85.

Statistical inference

Each child contributes many correlated minutes, so each outcome is modelled with a linear mixed model: cluster, hearing status and their interaction as fixed effects and a random intercept per child, with per-context Wald contrasts between HL and TH children. Random slopes would be under-identified with 13 children and six clusters. The homophily index, one value per child per context, is tested with Mann-Whitney U. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction is applied within each marker's six contrasts, as a family-wise correction would cost too much power in an exploratory study of this size.

The labels are post-hoc descriptions of the sensor profiles. All tests are run on cluster numbers. The contexts are qualitatively consistent with an independent manual coding of the daily routine, consulted only after K was fixed.

Peer co-presence

The difference changes sign between contexts. HL children are in groups more than TH children during independent work (+7.8% of the minute, d = +1.59, q < 0.0001), and less during peer-driven activity (−4.3%, q = 0.02) and seated guided work (−5.3%, q = 0.002). Pooled over the day the two groups look the same (38.8% against 39.9%), because these effects cancel out.

Vocal participation

HL children speak less where fast peer talk dominates and adult scaffolding is reduced: peer-driven activity (q = 0.034) and adult-scaffolded peer activity (q = 0.048). Where adult speech dominates or the room is quiet, the gap is small and not significant.

Peer affiliation

This is the most consistent asymmetry. TH children's grouped time is concentrated in TH-only groups, while HL children spend more grouped time in mixed groups than TH children in all six contexts, significantly in four. Class composition alone gives TH children a homophily advantage of about 0.08. In the four significant contexts the observed gap is 0.12 to 0.19, and it is as large in teacher-organised contexts as in child-chosen ones.

Discussion

Hypotheses revisited

  • H1, partly supported. Co-presence differences are context-specific, with significant effects of opposite sign. HL children exceeded TH children in the quiet independent-work context, as predicted, but fell below them in seated guided work, which the compensatory account did not anticipate.
  • H2, supported in direction. The gap was significant where peer speech dominated the room. The data suggest that the balance of peer and adult talk, rather than the teacher's distance, is the operative moderator. This refinement is post hoc and would need replication.
  • H3, first part supported. The affiliation asymmetry held in every context. The predicted moderation by who chooses the groups was not supported: the gap was as large in teacher-organised activities as in child-chosen ones.

Interpretation

Conditioning on context reveals effects that a whole-day comparison averages away, and the per-cluster pattern is more consistent with context-level than child-level drivers. The per-child random intercept matters for the same reason: a simpler ANOVA would report no effect of hearing status on vocal participation, while the mixed model shows that the signal is concentrated in specific contexts. With 13 children from one classroom, the findings are exploratory and call for replication in further cohorts. The results were presented to developmental psychologists.

Conclusions

  • Analyses should be conditioned on context, since effects of opposite sign can cancel out in a daily average.
  • Peer affiliation was the most consistent difference, with the same direction in all six contexts.
  • Selecting the number of clusters for stability rather than by BIC alone yielded contexts that were reproducible across random seeds.

The recordings of minors were collected under a research-ethics protocol that does not permit public release.
